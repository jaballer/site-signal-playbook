Revenue & performance measurement

A marketing site is a 24/7 salesperson. Most of them work unsupervised.

This is the information you have to gather before a marketing website can be managed as an asset instead of tolerated as a liability: what it's asked, what it answers, what it costs the visitor, and whether the numbers reporting all of that can be trusted. Written as a repeatable process for a technical owner working with marketing stakeholders.

Who it's for

The technical owner

The engineer who holds the site, and usually the tracking, the SEO and the reporting too. This defines the scope on day one instead of discovering it in month nine.

What it answers

The room's questions

How did they find us, where did they enter, what was the journey, where did they drop off, what converts, are we tracking everything, what's their intent, and what are AI assistants sending and saying?

What's in it

Nine chapters

Answer recipes for the questions leaders ask, the analyst-level question map, a six-phase sequence, a 48-signal catalog with capture specs, the tracking contract, three diagnostic flows, a reporting model, transferable patterns, and notes toward a health app.

Asset or liability

The difference is rarely design. It's whether the site's behaviour is observable, attributable and fast enough to close. The same page can sit on either side of this line.

Liability

  • Every form fires the same event, so no one can say which surface produces leads
  • Conversion numbers move and nobody can tell demand from a tagging change
  • New modules ship with no instrumentation; coverage decays release by release
  • Third-party scripts accumulate faster than they retire; nobody owns the budget
  • The conversion path carries seconds of avoidable delay that no one has timed
  • AI assistants answer questions about the product from third-party sites
  • Reporting is a monthly scramble that produces numbers nobody acts on

Asset

  • Every conversion names its form, its page and the CTA that sent it
  • Every number has a change log behind it, so a decline can be believed or dismissed
  • Instrumentation is a release checklist item with a named owner
  • Third parties are inventoried, classed and budgeted against load time
  • The conversion path is timed stage by stage and improved on purpose
  • The site is the entity AI assistants cite, with schema and machine-readable copy to match
  • A standing weekly view the team reads without being asked
“

Generic event names are an executive problem, not a developer problem. Renaming an event takes minutes; the discipline is naming it from the reporting question backward. form_submit answers nothing. form_submit carrying form variant, page and source CTA answers which form, on which page, from which CTA with no dashboard formula at all.

How to use this

When a leader asks something, open Recipes: each one is a step-by-step procedure with a sentence ready for the report. Use Questions to align with stakeholders on what they actually want to know, then work Phases and tick the checklist as you go; it persists in your browser. Use the Signal catalog to scope a layer and copy capture specs into the client's tracking plan. When a number looks wrong, open Diagnostics before opening a ticket. Reporting is the shape of the standing deliverable, and App notes lists the parts mechanical enough to become software.

Answer recipes

The questions leaders ask, and how to answer them

Step-by-step procedures for what a marketer, executive or leader expects the website to tell them. Each recipe covers who asks, what they expect to see, where to find it in GA4 and Search Console, how to read it, and a sentence to adapt for the report. On a new client, start with 00.

Every recipe runs the same loop
1TrustIs the number real? Check the tracking before reading it.
2SplitNever report a total alone. Break it down by form, page, channel or device.
3CompareAgainst the prior period, last year, or similar pages. Not a sitewide average.
4ExplainLine the change up against what shipped: site, tags, campaigns.
5ActOne change, an expected result, and a re-check next period.

Tracking contract

The event taxonomy every site should ship with

Three events, one UTM convention, one persistence cookie, and a short list of custom dimensions cover nearly every question in chapter two. Define them once, then never invent a new event inside a template.

Lead-journey status (bridges sessions without a warehouse)

Event-based analytics has no idea that this asset download and that form submission three weeks ago are the same person. A small first-party cookie with a five-value status field closes most of that gap for a fraction of the cost of a warehouse.

cookie_statusMeaningAttribution use

Internal CTA UTM convention

Internal CTAs that point at a conversion page on another host carry a fixed pattern: utm_campaign identifies the CTA's location and role, utm_content identifies the offer. Keep this strictly separate from external campaign UTMs — internal clicks tagged as campaigns pollute channel attribution, and the fix after the fact is a migration, not a setting.

CTAPattern
Global header?utm_source=website&utm_medium=cta&utm_campaign=global_header&utm_content=bookdemo
Homepage hero?utm_source=website&utm_medium=cta&utm_campaign=page_home_hero&utm_content=bookdemo
Blog post footer?utm_source=website&utm_medium=cta&utm_campaign=post_footer&utm_content=bookdemo&post={slug}

Custom dimensions to register (event scope)

form_variant, cta_position, post_slug, asset_id, source, cookie_status, is_new_cookie, content_group, logged_in. Register them the day the events ship — dimensions do not backfill, so a week's delay is a week of unusable data.

Persistence and hygiene

Campaign persistence cookie

A first-party cookie holding the URL-encoded original landing URL, so a visitor who reads three pages before submitting still carries attribution. A tag-manager variable decodes it for reporting and hidden-field injection.

Internal traffic exclusion

Exclude on CMS login state via a data-layer variable, not an IP list. Anyone testing in a clean browser bypasses it, so give contractors a QA cookie or a staging container and say so in writing.

One analytics module

Route every call through a single module — a generic trackEvent() with platform fallbacks, retry with backoff, and a promise result. Templates never call the analytics global directly, so the contract survives redesigns.

Change log as audit trail

Name every tag-manager version for the intervention it makes. When the work never touches the repo, that history is the engineering record — and the thing that lets you tell a real decline from a tagging artifact.

Diagnostic flows

When a number looks wrong

Three situations that recur on every marketing site. Each is a fixed order of checks, because the cheapest checks are the ones that most often end the investigation — and because the expensive ones produce confident wrong answers when run first.

What this looks like in the field: leadership arrives with a single aggregate — "form conversions are down by a third, we missed the quarter." Split by form, the picture usually inverts: a newly added popup is growing while two page-embedded forms fall away, and the aggregate is just the net. Meanwhile click-event volume spikes on exactly the days the tag container was published. Both facts are invisible in the aggregate, and both change what you should fix.
Validate the baseline before the decline

Overlay daily event volume on the change log — tag publishes, plugin installs, deploys. Spikes within one to three days of a change are testing or duplicate firing, not demand. Caveat any decline whose baseline window contains them.

Check for duplicate firing

Run tag assistant on the conversion and thank-you pages: tags reported as firing twice mean conversions are double-counted after every publish. In the network tab, count instances of the analytics loader and each third-party tag per page.

Break the aggregate by form variant

Split by form identifier or thank-you query string. A uniform decline points at a sitewide cause — performance, tracking, consent. A concentrated one points at specific source pages, and those are two entirely different investigations.

Split traffic loss from conversion-rate loss

For each declining variant: sessions to its host pages versus submissions per session. Traffic loss is a channel and SEO question; conversion-rate loss is a page question — copy, competing CTAs, form load, a new popup stealing the click.

Test for cannibalization

Count users who submitted any form. If that's flat while the mix shifted toward a new surface, nothing was lost — it moved, and the "decline" is a reporting artifact of looking at one form.

Only then look at performance and the CRM

Form error logging, server logs for the form endpoint, and CRM-side sync failures. All real causes, all worth instrumenting, and all rarely the first one.

What this looks like in the field: the field-data report shows mobile "good" URLs falling from most of the site to zero inside two weeks, then staying there for a month while nobody notices. Several deployments sit in that window — a new popup, a security plugin, two tracking scripts — and the caching plugin turns out to have been left in safe mode, meaning every trace you take is measuring an unoptimized site rather than the one you thought you were tuning.
Confirm which metric failed, on which device

Field data by device, plus origin-level data for context. LCP, INP and CLS fail for unrelated reasons; "collapsed" almost always means one of them, and fixing the other two wastes the quarter.

Build the regression timeline

Every plugin install, tag publish, theme deploy and hosting change in the window, on one line. Correlation with the collapse date is the cheapest hypothesis generator available.

Check the optimization layer is actually on

Caching plugin safe mode, CDN status, minify and combine, lazy loading. The single biggest lever is often re-enabling something that was switched off during an unrelated incident and never switched back.

Cold-cache network trace of the top template and the conversion page

Incognito, cache disabled. Record TTFB, render-blocking count and bytes, third-party hosts, duplicate loads, and total wire weight by resource type. Note that browser-trace numbers are not throttled-mobile numbers — report both, and say which is which.

Inventory third parties and look for redundancy

Marketing stacks accumulate several tools doing the same job — two or three visitor-deanonymization scripts, overlapping session recorders, a chat widget shipping a full UI on load. Consolidation usually beats any single code fix, and it needs marketing-ops sign-off, so start that conversation early.

Find cache bypasses on money pages

Query strings on thank-you and campaign URLs commonly bypass the page cache by default, leaving the most conversion-critical page as the slowest one on the site. Often a one-line config fix worth hundreds of milliseconds.

Tier the fixes, ship one at a time, measure after each

Tier 0: config changes reversible in minutes. Tier 1: preloads, image formats, preconnects, font loading. Tier 2: dequeue unused CSS and JS, icon fonts, video facades, lazy iframes. Record expected versus actual impact — a large miss means the diagnosis was wrong, not the fix.

What this looks like in the field: a stakeholder reports "a couple of seconds" between submitting the form and seeing the thank-you page. Traced stage by stage, it's four things stacked: a chat tool holding the submit to attempt an intercept, a consent manager holding tags for a fixed wait, an uncached thank-you page, and a cross-origin form iframe negotiating a fresh connection on open. Most of it is configuration, not code.
Time each stage separately

Modal open → form ready; submit → provider response; response → redirect; redirect → thank-you first paint. One stopwatch number is a complaint, not a diagnosis.

Look for built-in waits

Chat tools intercept form submits on a configurable threshold; consent managers hold tag firing for a fixed window. Both ship with generous defaults, and both are usually set once and never revisited.

Check the destination's TTFB

Thank-you pages carrying query strings are frequently uncached. Add the parameter to the cache allow-list and confirm nothing on the page depends on per-request rendering.

Preconnect to the form's origin

One preconnect hint to the form host removes DNS, TCP and TLS from the modal open — and benefits the submit round-trip on the same origin.

Separate UX delay from CWV

Submit delay is a UX problem measured in seconds; form load is a Core Web Vitals problem measured in LCP and INP. Track and report them separately, or they get conflated in the room and the wrong one gets the budget.

Add feedback even after fixing it

A disabled button and a spinner on submit cuts double-submissions and perceived wait. Additive to the real fix, never a substitute for it.

The attribution ceiling

"Which CTA led to which submission?" is a completely standard question that the default analytics-plus-dashboard stack under-delivers on: an event-based model with no session or user joins, brittle blends, cross-domain page paths, and no native funnel support. Say this in the first review rather than the fourth. Two honest options:

Option A

Stay in the dashboard tool

Custom events plus a blend joining page path to referrer. It works, it's manual, and it breaks silently on URL mismatches. Adequate for a team that needs directional CTA performance and nothing more.

Option B

Warehouse export

Export raw events, join clicks and submissions on session or user ID in SQL, visualize anywhere. Solid, and the only way to do spike-source analysis or true path work. Costs setup and SQL time. Recommend it for any team that wants real attribution.

Naming the ceiling honestly builds more credibility than promising to try harder in the dashboard tool — and it turns a recurring disappointment into a budget decision the client can actually make.
