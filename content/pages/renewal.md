heading: Keeping the account, and knowing when not to
lede: "The quarterly review is a renewal conversation whether or not anyone calls it one. This chapter covers how that conversation is prepared and run, the signs an account is drifting long before anyone says so, what to do when results are behind, how scope changes are priced, and how an engagement ends well enough to come back."
blocks:
  - type: thesis
    mark: →
    title: Renewals are won in month two, not month eleven.
    text: A client renews because the program has been legible all year, the numbers have been trustworthy every month, and nothing in the review is a surprise. Nothing said in the renewal meeting can fix a year the client couldn't follow. By then the decision is usually already made, and the meeting only confirms it.
  - type: flow
    heading: Running the quarterly review
    intro: "The [Review & renew](phase:review-renew) phase, as a conversation. Prepare it as though the budget holder has forgotten everything since the last one, because they usually have."
    items:
      - title: Do the work two weeks out
        text: "Re-run the [prompt set](term:prompt-set), re-benchmark competitors, re-score the audit pillars that moved, and re-run the opportunity model on what's actually known now. Every number in the deck should be one you'd defend to a CFO."
      - title: Write the answer before the deck
        text: "One paragraph, in plain words: what the quarter produced, what it cost, what it means for next quarter. If that paragraph is hard to write, the deck won't fix it, and the difficulty is telling you something."
      - title: Send the numbers ahead of the meeting
        text: Give the budget holder the results a day early so they arrive with questions instead of reactions. A leader reading a decline for the first time in a live meeting will defend themselves rather than think.
      - title: Present results against the targets that were set, not new ones
        text: "Use the target ranges from the roadmap, with the [expected impact written before the work started](principle:write-the-expected-impact-first). Where a play missed, say so and say what the miss taught. Quietly re-cutting the targets is the fastest way to lose a technical buyer."
      - title: Make the ask specific
        text: Renew at the same scope, expand into a named area, or change what's being funded. Bring the revenue case for each, and name the one you recommend. A review that ends with "let us know" ends with nothing.
      - title: Record the decision and the date
        text: Whatever is agreed goes in the decisions log the same day, along with anything the client committed to. Next quarter's review starts from that record.
  - type: table
    heading: The signs an account is drifting
    intro: Churn is almost never announced. It shows up in the working relationship a quarter or two before it shows up in a cancellation, and every one of these is recoverable if it's named early.
    columns:
      - What you notice
      - What it usually means
      - What to do about it
    rows:
      - - The budget holder stops coming to the monthly call
        - It's been delegated, which means it's stopped being a priority they defend internally
        - Ask for fifteen minutes with them directly, and bring one thing that needs their decision. Delegated accounts get cut without a conversation.
      - - Approvals and expert time slip month after month
        - The client's team is overloaded, or nobody internally owns the work
        - "Show the cost of the delay in the roadmap: what's held, and what it's worth. Then either get a named owner or cut the scope to what their team can actually feed."
      - - Reports get read but never produce a decision
        - The report isn't answering a question anyone has
        - Ask the marketing leader what they're being asked by their own leadership, and rebuild the summary around that. A report nobody acts on is a report nobody will miss.
      - - A new marketing leader arrives
        - Every agency relationship resets, and the new leader owes the old one nothing
        - Get in front of them in the first month with the audit, the baseline and the results so far. Assume nothing has been handed over, because it hasn't.
      - - The conversation turns to deliverable counts
        - They've stopped believing in the outcome and started counting the inputs
        - Go back to the opportunity model and the chain from visibility to pipeline. If they won't engage with it, the account is already commodity work and priced wrong.
      - - Procurement or finance appears
        - A cost review is running, and every line is being justified
        - Send the pipeline case in the format finance uses, without being asked. Agencies that make finance's job easy survive cost reviews.
      - - Nobody has mentioned the competitor benchmark in two quarters
        - The program has drifted into maintenance
        - Re-run the benchmark and lead the next review with it. Competitive loss is the thing leadership reacts to fastest.
  - type: panels
    heading: When the results are behind
    intro: The conversation most agency relationships die in, usually because it happened too late and sounded like an excuse.
    items:
      - eyebrow: Do this
        title: Raise it the month you see it
        text: The moment a play misses its first-signal date by a clear margin, say so in that month's report, with what you think happened and what you're changing. A miss reported early reads as rigor. The same miss surfaced at the quarterly review reads as concealment.
      - eyebrow: Do this
        title: Separate what was shipped from what was held
        text: Work that waited on the client's approvals, expert time or releases is not the same as work that shipped and didn't land, and the two need different responses. The sprint notes and the roadmap already record which is which, which is why they're kept that way.
      - eyebrow: Do this
        title: Name the diagnosis that was wrong
        text: "A large miss usually means the diagnosis was wrong, not that the execution was lazy. Say which assumption failed and what it's worth knowing. That's the [point of writing the expected impact down first](principle:write-the-expected-impact-first)."
      - eyebrow: Not this
        title: Don't move to a metric that looks better
        text: Switching the headline from pipeline to impressions in the quarter pipeline fell is the single most damaging thing an agency can do to its own credibility, and technical buyers spot it immediately. Report the metric that was agreed, then explain it.
  - type: ledger
    heading: Renew, or end it
    intro: Not every account should be renewed, and an agency that renews everything ends up funding its worst clients with the margin from its best.
    left:
      title: Signs to end or reshape it
      items:
        - The client's team can't feed the work, and three quarters of held sprints prove it
        - The fee has stopped making sense against what the program can realistically return
        - The relationship runs on complaints rather than decisions
        - Results depend on changes the client won't release, and that's been true all year
        - They want volume at a price that means doing the work badly
        - The account only survives because one person likes you
    right:
      title: Signs to renew or expand
      items:
        - The chain from visibility to pipeline moved, and the client can explain it in their own words
        - Approvals, expert time and releases arrive roughly on schedule
        - The budget holder brings their own questions to the review
        - Competitive position improved on the benchmark, not only the client's own numbers
        - There's a named next area worth more than the current scope
        - They've referred someone, or asked whether you have capacity
  - type: table
    heading: Scope changes
    intro: Most margin is lost to work nobody agreed to and nobody billed for. The rule is that new work gets written down before it starts, however small it is.
    columns:
      - Situation
      - How it's handled
    rows:
      - - A small request outside scope, first time
        - Do it, and say in writing that it was out of scope and why it was absorbed. The record matters more than the hour.
      - - The same small request, repeatedly
        - It's a pattern, not a favour. Name it at the monthly call and either add it to scope for a fee or stop doing it.
      - - A new project the client wants alongside the program
        - Scope and price it separately, with its own start date. Never fold it into the retainer to keep the peace; it dilutes everything the retainer was funding.
      - - A change of priorities mid-quarter
        - Fine, and expected. Swap something out of the roadmap rather than adding to it, and record the trade in the decisions log so nobody remembers it differently later.
      - - Work blocked on the client for more than a sprint
        - Hold it, show it as held, and offer to swap in something not blocked. Don't quietly do something else and don't quietly do nothing.
      - - A request for tactics that break platform rules
        - "Decline in writing, once, without a lecture, and explain the risk to them rather than to us: delisting, a manual action, or public embarrassment."
  - type: strip
    heading: Ending well
    intro: Some engagements should end, and how they end decides whether the client comes back or warns other people off. Handle the last month as carefully as the first.
    items:
      - title: Hand over everything
        text: The measurement setup record, the change log, the keyword and prompt sets, the roadmap and every specification. Leaving a client dependent doesn't retain them, it just guarantees they say so later.
      - title: Say what's unfinished
        text: A short, honest note on what was in flight, what it would take to finish, and what will decay if nobody maintains it.
      - title: Ask what went wrong, and listen
        text: A fifteen-minute call with the budget holder, with no proposal in it. This is the cheapest account research available, and the only kind that's honest.
      - title: Keep the door open on a date
        text: Agree to check in once, at a specific point, usually a quarter out. Vague promises to stay in touch don't produce anything.
      - title: Watch for the trigger to come back
        text: "A win-back almost always follows an event rather than a nurture sequence: a new marketing leader, a redesign, a traffic drop or a competitor pulling ahead in AI answers. Have the observation ready before you call."
    footnote: "**Work that's already been done for them is the best reason to reopen a conversation.** A re-run of their prompt set against competitors costs an hour and is more persuasive than any follow-up email."
